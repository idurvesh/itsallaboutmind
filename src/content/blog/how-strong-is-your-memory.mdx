Priya was standing in the kitchen with her keys in her hand, trying to remember why she'd walked in. It happens most days now. She can recite her best friend's childhood phone number without trying, but she forgot her own cousin's name at a wedding last month.

That gap between old memory and new memory is common. Some of it is attention. Some of it is habit. A short memory quiz will not diagnose anything, but it can show you where your recall tends to be strong and where it tends to slip.

This quiz asks about the everyday moments where memory shows up: names, routines, directions, and small details. Answer honestly, based on how you actually behave, not how you wish you behaved. Six questions, two minutes, one score.

## What your result means

**Memory in progress.** A lower score does not mean a weak memory. It usually means your recall depends heavily on structure. If you write things down, set a routine, or repeat a name out loud, you remember it well. The gap shows up when there's no structure to lean on. That's a habit issue more than a capacity issue.

**Steady recall.** This is the most common result. Your memory is reliable when you're focused, and it slips when you're doing three things at once. Most people in this band remember more than they give themselves credit for. The forgetting happens in the noisy, distracted moments, not the quiet ones.

**Sharp recall.** You hold detail well under normal conditions, from names to sequences to directions. That's a genuine strength, though it doesn't mean you're immune to the same distraction-driven slips as everyone else. It just takes more to throw you off.

Wherever you landed, the score is a snapshot of one ordinary week, not a fixed trait. Attention, sleep, and stress all move it around.
