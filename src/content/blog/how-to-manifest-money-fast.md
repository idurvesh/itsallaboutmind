Vikas ran a small printing shop in Indore. Every night before bed, he sat on the edge of his cot and said the same line out loud: "Money is coming to me easily."

He had read it in a book. He believed it, mostly. He said it for four months.

His shop stayed exactly as broke as before.

One evening his wife, Pooja, overheard him whispering the line for the hundredth time. She laughed, not unkindly, and asked, "Easily from where? You have not raised your printing rates in three years because you feel bad charging people more."

Vikas opened his mouth to argue. Then he closed it again.

She was right. He had been saying "money is coming easily" every single night while quietly making sure it could not.

That is what a money block looks like. It is not laziness. It is not bad luck. It is a quiet belief sitting underneath your affirmations, working against every word you say.

Here are the five most common ones, and what to do about each.

## Block 1: You Think Wanting Money Is a Bit Dirty

A lot of us grew up hearing some version of "money is not everything" or "good people do not chase money." Useful advice against greed. Terrible advice if you actually need rent money.

Meera, a schoolteacher in Kochi, told me she felt physically uncomfortable asking parents for late fee payments. "I would rather lose the money than feel like I am being greedy," she said.

She was not greedy. She was underpaid and overgiving, and she had quietly decided that wanting more money made her a worse person.

The fix is not complicated, just uncomfortable at first. Say this out loud, even if it feels strange: "Wanting money does not make me a bad person. I can be generous and well paid at the same time."

Meera started saying that line every morning for three weeks. She also, finally, sent a polite reminder text to two parents who owed her fees. Both paid within a day. She had been avoiding a five-minute text for over a year because of a belief she picked up before she was ten years old.

## Block 2: You Do Not Think You Deserve It

This one hides better than the first. It does not sound like "money is bad." It sounds like "I will ask for more once I am better at my job" or "maybe next year, once I have proven myself."

Lisa, a graphic designer in Atlanta, undercharged every single client for six years. She would quote a fair price in her head, then knock 30 percent off before sending the email, every time, without fail.

When her friend finally asked why, Lisa said, "What if they think I am not worth it?"

Her friend asked a better question back: "What if you are not worth it because you have never once tested the price you actually deserve?"

Lisa raised her rates on her very next project. Nervous the entire time. The client said yes without a single question.

Worth is not something you earn by waiting quietly. It is something you decide, and then the world tends to agree faster than you expect.

## Block 3: You Are Manifesting "Rich," Not a Number

This is the block that wrecks the most plans, because it feels productive while doing nothing.

"I want to be rich" is not a goal. It is a mood. Your brain cannot act on a mood.

Rohit, a delivery rider in Bengaluru, wanted to manifest money for almost a year using exactly this kind of vague wish. Nothing changed. Then a friend asked him a simple question: "Rich by when, and how much?"

Rohit had never actually answered that for himself. He sat down and got specific: forty thousand extra rupees in ninety days, to clear a phone loan and start a small savings buffer.

Suddenly he had something to aim at. He picked up two extra delivery shifts a week. He noticed a referral bonus his app offered that he had scrolled past for months. He hit his number in eleven weeks, not ninety days. Close enough that he still tells the story.

A number with a deadline gives your brain something to work toward. "I want to be rich" gives it nothing to do but feel anxious.

## Block 4: You Are Waiting for the Universe to Do Your Job

Manifestation is not a replacement for effort. It is a way of pointing your effort in the right direction.

Karthik, an engineering student from a small town in Tamil Nadu, wrote "I am getting a paid internship" in his journal every day for two months. He also did not update his resume once, did not apply anywhere, and did not message a single senior from his college for advice.

He was frustrated that "nothing was happening."

His sister finally said something blunt: "You are writing wishes into a diary and hoping a company finds the diary."

He laughed, because it was true and it stung a little. He spent that weekend updating his resume and messaging four seniors. Two replied. One forwarded his resume internally. He had a paid internship within three weeks, and it came from the exact senior who almost did not reply because Karthik's first message was too vague.

Writing things down sets your intention. Action is what turns the intention into money. You need both. Skipping the second one is the single most common reason people feel stuck.

## Block 5: You Are Watching Your Bank Balance Like It Owes You an Apology

Checking your account fifteen times a day while feeling anxious is not "staying aware of your finances." It is rehearsing scarcity, over and over, until your body believes that is the only state available to it.

Tom, an accountant in Manchester, admitted he checked his banking app so often that his phone showed it as his most-used app three weeks running. Every check made his chest tighten a little, even on days his balance was perfectly fine.

He did not stop checking his money. He just changed how often, and added one rule: check once a day, in the morning, and say one neutral or kind sentence about it no matter what the number is. Even on a bad day, he would say, "This is where I am right now. It is going to move."

Three months later his anxiety around money had dropped enough that his partner noticed before he did. His income had not changed yet. His relationship with the number had. That came first, and the income followed not long after, once he started negotiating a raise he had been too anxious to ask for.

## A Simple Daily Money Practice You Can Actually Keep

You do not need a vision board covered in gold coins to start. Five minutes a day is enough.

1. **Write your number.** One sentence, one specific amount, one rough deadline. "I am bringing in an extra fifteen thousand rupees by the end of next month."
2. **Add one line of permission.** Something that pushes back on whichever block sounds most like you. "I deserve to be paid fairly for my work."
3. **Do one money action today.** Send the invoice you have been avoiding. Apply for the role. Ask about the raise. Sell the thing sitting in your cupboard for six months. Small counts.
4. **Check your balance once, calmly.** Say something neutral about the number, even if it is not where you want it yet.
5. **Notice one small win.** A coupon. An unexpected refund. A client paying on time for once. Write it down. Your brain needs the evidence.

Do this for thirty days before deciding whether it works. Vikas, the printing shop owner from the start of this post, finally raised his rates after Pooja's comment and added this five-minute practice. Six weeks later his monthly income was up by almost a third. Not because the universe rearranged itself overnight, but because he stopped quietly sabotaging his own affirmation every single night without realizing it.

## What to Do Once Money Actually Shows Up

This part gets skipped constantly, and it matters more than people think.

When money finally comes in, after weeks of feeling stuck, a strange thing happens to a lot of people. They panic and spend it immediately, almost like they are scared it will vanish if they do not use it fast. Or they feel guilty enough to give most of it away before they have even let themselves enjoy it.

Pause for a moment when money arrives. Say thank you, even if it feels silly saying it to no one in particular. Put a little aside before spending any of it, even if it is a small amount. This single habit trains your brain to expect more money to follow, instead of treating every bit of income like a fluke that needs to be used up before it disappears.

## Which Money Block Is Actually Yours

Most people have one block doing most of the damage, not all five equally. Maybe you undercharge like Lisa. Maybe you write wishes without action like Karthik. Maybe you are stuck checking your balance with a knot in your stomach like Tom used to.

Once you know which one is yours, fixing it gets a lot faster, because you stop guessing and start working on the actual problem instead of every problem at once.

## Start With One Honest Question

Before you write another affirmation about money, ask yourself one honest question: which of these five blocks sounds the most like you?

Vikas's answer was guilt about charging fairly. Lisa's was thinking she was not worth the higher rate. Karthik's was writing wishes without lifting a finger to back them up.

Find yours. Say the opposite of it out loud tomorrow morning. Then do one small, real thing about it before lunch.

That is the whole method. No crystals required, though you are welcome to keep yours on the desk if they make you feel good while you do the actual work.
